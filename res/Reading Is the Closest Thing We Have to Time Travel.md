# Reading Is the Closest Thing We Have to Time Travel

There is something strange about reading.

You sit alone in a room, staring at marks on a page, and somehow a voice appears inside your head.

Not your voice.

Someone else's.

A person who may have lived 500 years ago, spoken a different language, lived on another continent, and died long before you were born can suddenly occupy your mind for an afternoon.

They can make you angry.

They can change your opinion.

They can make you see the world differently.

And then, when you close the book, they disappear.

Reading might be the most ordinary form of time travel we've invented.

### The peculiar technology of books

Think about what a book actually does.

A writer takes something that exists inside their mind and converts it into symbols.

You look at those symbols and reconstruct the idea inside your own mind.

That's an astonishing piece of technology.

When you read a sentence like:

> “He stood at the edge of the cliff.”

You don't merely decode the words.

Your brain constructs a cliff.

You imagine the height. The wind. The person standing there. Perhaps even the feeling in your stomach as you look down.

The author provides a handful of words.

Your brain builds an entire world.

In that sense, reading isn't really consuming information.

**It's collaborating with the author to create an experience.**

And perhaps that's why reading feels so different from watching something.

A movie shows you the character.

A book makes you build the character.

A video gives you the scenery.

A book makes your imagination render it.

Reading leaves some of the work to you.

And that work is precisely what makes it powerful.

---

### The problem with modern reading

But something has changed.

We read more than ever, yet somehow we read less.

Messages.

Notifications.

Tweets.

News headlines.

Instagram captions.

Search results.

Product descriptions.

Ten tabs open simultaneously.

Our eyes move constantly, but our minds rarely stay in one place for long.

This is a very different kind of reading.

Most digital reading is designed for **extraction**.

What's the point?

What's the answer?

What happened?

Should I click this?

Can I move on?

Books ask for something else.

They ask you to stay.

To tolerate a sentence that doesn't immediately reward you.

To follow an argument for twenty pages before reaching its conclusion.

To sit with an unfamiliar idea without immediately agreeing or disagreeing with it.

In other words, reading books trains something increasingly rare:

**sustained attention.**

---

### Reading changes the shape of your thoughts

There is another reason reading matters.

The quality of your thoughts is partly determined by the concepts available to you.

If your vocabulary for describing an experience is simply:

*"I feel bad."*

then many different emotional states get compressed into the same tiny box.

But once you encounter words like *melancholy, resentment, anticipation, nostalgia, alienation, awe,* or *ambivalence*, your internal world becomes more detailed.

You can distinguish things you previously experienced as one vague feeling.

The same thing happens with ideas.

Before reading about economics, you might see rising prices simply as "things becoming expensive."

After learning about inflation, supply constraints, monetary policy and purchasing power, you have several new lenses through which to interpret the same event.

Reading gives you **mental vocabulary**.

And mental vocabulary gives you more ways to think.

---

### The best books don't give you answers

This is perhaps the biggest misconception about reading.

People often approach books like containers of information.

*"What will I get from this book?"*

But the most valuable books often don't give you answers.

They give you better questions.

You finish a great book and discover that the world has become slightly more complicated.

Things that once seemed obvious now seem questionable.

People you previously judged seem more understandable.

Opinions you held confidently acquire footnotes.

You start noticing patterns that were invisible before.

That's not knowledge in the conventional sense.

It's something closer to **expanded perception**.

---

### You don't need to read 50 books a year

There is also a strange productivity culture around reading.

People track books.

They post reading lists.

They set yearly targets.

*"I read 73 books this year."*

But reading isn't a race.

If you spend three months slowly working through one book that permanently changes how you think, it may be worth more than racing through thirty books you barely remember.

The goal isn't to maximize the number of books you finish.

It's to maximize the number of ideas that stay with you.

Some books should be consumed quickly.

Some should be studied.

Some should be argued with.

Some should be abandoned.

And some should be read twice.

Because sometimes you don't reread a book because you forgot what it said.

You reread it because **you've changed since the first time you read it.**

The words stayed the same.

You didn't.

---

### A book is a conversation with someone who isn't there

Perhaps this is the most beautiful thing about reading.

A book is a conversation where the other person cannot interrupt you.

You can disagree with Aristotle.

Argue with Nietzsche.

Laugh at Dickens.

Question Darwin.

Learn from Einstein.

Follow the thoughts of someone who lived centuries ago.

You can enter the mind of a person without needing an invitation.

And unlike a conversation, you can pause.

Underline a sentence.

Go back ten pages.

Think for an hour.

Close the book.

Return tomorrow.

The author waits.

Few technologies are this patient.

---

### So why read?

Not because successful people read.

Not because intelligent people read.

Not because someone on the internet told you that reading will make you smarter.

Read because your life is limited to one perspective.

Your own.

You get one childhood.

One set of experiences.

One brain.

One lifetime.

Books allow you to temporarily borrow other people's lives.

You can experience a war you never fought.

A country you've never visited.

A century you never lived through.

A profession you never practiced.

A mind nothing like your own.

And when you return to your own life, you bring some of those perspectives with you.

Maybe that's what reading really is.

Not escaping your life.

**Expanding it.**

You still have only one lifetime.

But if you read deeply enough, it can contain many.

And that might be the closest thing humanity has ever invented to time travel.
